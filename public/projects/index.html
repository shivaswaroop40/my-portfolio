Thesis and side projects

Enforcement-readiness of generated Kubernetes NetworkPolicies

Master thesis · Ankra · 2026

The problem

Plenty of tools can watch a cluster and generate a NetworkPolicy that blocks attacks. None of them tell you whether that policy is safe to turn on without breaking the application. The papers do not check either. They evaluate a policy against the same traffic the tool observed, so a missing edge never shows up.

What I built

Two things. The first is a benchmark that works with any generator. It injects real attacks and scores the resulting policy on block-rate, over-privilege and false-denies. The second is a prototype that fuses eBPF flow observation from Cilium Hubble with dependencies read from cluster configuration: Services, Ingress, DNS, RBAC and StatefulSet peers.

What came out of it

Watching traffic alone tops out at around 72% of the edges an application needs. Adding configuration raises that to around 82% and cuts false-denies, with over-privilege held at zero. Everything was measured on a live k3s and Cilium cluster, against the Ankra control plane and Sock Shop.

Sift

Side project · updated daily

The problem

Keeping up with a field means reading a firehose of feeds, and most of what is in them is a product launch or a rewrite of yesterday's story. I wanted the dozen items a day that are actually worth the time, per field, without doing the reading myself.

What I built

A script that runs every morning in GitHub Actions. For each field it reads every feed, 65 of them across four fields, drops what it has seen before and asks an LLM to score each new item on depth, novelty and usefulness to one specific reader. Near-duplicate stories are collapsed, each source is capped and the top dozen get a short summary written from the article text. The output is plain markdown in the repository, built into a static site with Astro. Each field has a front page, an archive and an RSS feed.

It runs on a cheap open model with a second model as fallback. An edition is written whole or not at all, so a crash halfway leaves yesterday's edition in place. The site deploys to GitHub Pages, and shivu.io serves the same pages under /sift through the edge worker.

What came out of it

Four daily editions: tech, cybersecurity, chemical engineering and travel. A day is roughly 75 short model calls and takes under ten minutes.

containerImages

Open source

The problem

Signing and scanning container images is only worth something if the cluster refuses whatever skipped the process. Most examples stop at the pipeline and never show the refusal.

What I built

A supply-chain pipeline you can copy. Every push builds a multi-arch image with Buildx and pushes it to GHCR. Trivy scans the pushed digest, and a critical or high finding fails the job, so a vulnerable image never gets a signature. Trivy then generates a CycloneDX SBOM. Cosign signs the digest keyless, using GitHub's OIDC token for a short-lived Fulcio certificate, and attests the SBOM against the same digest. There are no key secrets in the repository. Everything after the build refers to the image by digest, because a signature on a tag is a signature on whatever the tag points at today.

On the cluster, a Kyverno ClusterPolicy verifies the signature and its Fulcio identity, requires the SBOM attestation and rejects anything still referenced by bare tag.

What came out of it

The demo is two images from the same Dockerfile. One goes through the pipeline and the other is pushed as it is. The cluster runs the first and rejects the second. I built it for my talk at the Stockholm Cloud Native meetup and still use it for my own images.

Ensembles of LLMs on one GPU

Communication System Design project · six people · January 2026

The problem

A single small language model is uneven across domains, and a large one does not fit on modest hardware. We asked which way of combining several small models works best when all you have is one Nvidia T4 with 16 GB of memory, and whether the combination can approach a larger model.

What we built

We first benchmarked seven open models on four datasets: GSM8K for maths, CNN/DailyMail for summarisation, WMT14 for translation and ARC for reasoning. We then built five ensembles on top of the five best models. Two route a query to one model before inference, one with a trained neural router and one with K-Means clusters. One votes word by word during generation. Two work after inference: a cascade that stops as soon as enough models agree, and a fusion pipeline where one model merges the others' answers. In the project plan my share, with one teammate, was the benchmarking pipeline, GPU efficiency and the two after-inference ensembles.

What came out of it

No technique won everywhere. Picking the best single model per query, which is the ceiling for any router, scored 0.87 on our composite metric. The trained router reached 0.80 and added about 0.01 seconds. Fusion scored 0.86 but took 4.3 seconds on average, too slow for interactive use. The cascade stopped after two models 57% of the time, and that was its weakness: models often agreed on an answer that was similarly off, and the best model was among those consulted in only 44% of cases. Memory set the hard limit. The five models in float16 take about 12 GB, which leaves 4 GB for everything else.

Securing a two-site company network

Building Networked Systems Security · four people · 2025

The problem

A fictional company, ACME, has a headquarters in Stockholm, a branch in London and people working remotely. Users and devices must prove who they are, traffic between the offices must be unreadable on the way, a lost laptop must be locked out and someone has to notice an intrusion.

What we built

A working lab network on virtual machines, from a requirements and design document through to the implementation. OpenWrt runs on both routers. A PKI built with Easy-RSA issues X.509 certificates to servers, users and routers, with a revocation list for compromised clients. OpenVPN carries the site-to-site tunnel, with Stockholm as server and London as client, so London users reach Stockholm services by private address. Both routers drop traffic by default and allow by exception with iptables. Snort watches mirrored traffic for DDoS, SQL injection and similar patterns, and sends alerts to Graylog, which also collects VPN, web server and firewall logs over TLS. Nextcloud with time-based one-time passwords handles file exchange and chat, and an Apache web server authenticates against OpenLDAP.

What came out of it

A running end-to-end environment and a design that survived a round of peer review. The honest limitation is in the PKI. Revocation is dynamic, but adding a new employee still means creating certificates by hand. The report has no measurements, so I will not claim any.

Cyber risk in euros with YACRAF

Security Analysis of Large-Scale Computer Systems · solo · January 2026

The problem

The case is a fitness business with eight studios in Stockholm. The studios run legacy on-premise door access, while the revenue depends on booking, payment and streaming services hosted on AWS. The question was how much cyber risk that carries in euros, and whether a defence budget pays for itself.

What I did

A full assessment with YACRAF, the risk assessment framework taught in the course. It covers two threat actor profiles, four abuse cases (cloned entry cards, ransomware, payment fraud through the API and account takeover), data flow diagrams, attack trees, a trust boundary model and mappings to MITRE ATT&CK techniques and mitigations. Risk is quantified as loss event frequency times loss magnitude, with return on investment, net present value and payback period on top.

What came out of it

The model puts annual exposure at about EUR 934,000, and almost nine tenths of it is ransomware. Eight defences costing EUR 119,500 in the first year bring the modelled loss down to about EUR 50,000. These are estimates from assumed frequencies, not measurements, and the ranking matters more than the exact figures. I also wrote a reflection on how the assessment's own assumptions can discriminate: a stereotyped attacker, mandatory Face ID and MFA that shut out people with disabilities, and a user assumed to speak Swedish or English.

Wi-Fi models against measurements

Wireless networks course project · three people

The problem

Propagation models predict how a Wi-Fi signal fades indoors. We wanted to know how far those predictions are from what a device receives in a real office, and whether 802.11ad at 60 GHz is a better indoor option than 802.11ac at 2.4 and 5 GHz.

What we did

We surveyed an office of about 283 square metres, divided by glass and wooden partitions and served by four 802.11ac routers. Signal strength was averaged over several readings at fixed points and compared with the ITU-R indoor propagation model. Heatmaps came from TamoGraph for 802.11ac and MATLAB for 802.11ad. For 60 GHz we moved an access point through the space and read the signal from its dashboard. My part was the literature review across the three bands, the measurement plan and where the points went, the 2.4 and 5 GHz measurements, checking the collected data, and the discussion and future work sections.

What came out of it

Measured loss was higher than the model predicted, which we put down to furniture, reflections from metal and interference on 2.4 GHz. The 2.4 GHz band covered more of the office at lower peak strength, and 5 GHz was stronger over short distances. At 60 GHz the usable range was about 10.9 metres and needed a clear line of sight. Beamforming helped on direct paths and did little against obstacles, so we judged it a poor fit for covering a whole office.

Designing a small ISP

IK2215 network design · two people

The problem

Design the network of a small ISP, a single autonomous system in a course lab. It has to survive link failures, prefer one upstream provider and keep a second as backup, never carry traffic between the two, and host its own DNS, web and DHCP services.

What we designed

Four routers, three servers and two clients, with an address plan down to every interface. OSPF runs inside the network, with link costs chosen so that every router has at least two disjoint paths. BGP faces the two upstreams. The network advertises one aggregated prefix, an iBGP session between the two border routers runs over loopback addresses, and local preference of 150 against 50 steers traffic to the primary link. The policy keeps either upstream from using the network as transit and keeps it reachable if one border router goes down. DNS is BIND 9, and the router nearest the clients relays DHCP.

What came out of it

A design specification, and a peer review I wrote of another group's design against the same criteria. The report itself contains no measurements.

Do smart appliances save energy?

II2202 research methodology · two people · autumn 2024

The problem

Smart home devices are sold on convenience and efficiency, and there are few comparisons of what they do to a household's energy use.

What we did

A case study. We collected energy ratings and power figures for smart appliances from manufacturers' product pages, and for older traditional models from service manuals, and discarded values that conflicted. We then costed three households, a single person, a couple and a family of four, once with traditional and once with smart versions of five appliances: TV, fridge, washing machine, microwave and LED bulbs.

What came out of it

In all three scenarios the smart set used roughly half the energy. It is a rough answer, and the report says why. Public data was thin, the usage patterns were invented rather than surveyed, and the carbon footprint comparison we had planned, including manufacturing, was not finished.
